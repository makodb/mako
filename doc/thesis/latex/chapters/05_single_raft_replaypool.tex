\chapter{Single-Raft and ReplayPool}

Chapter 3 explained how Single-Raft consolidates Mako's worker-lane commands
into one ordered Raft log, with one local replica of that consolidated group
running in each process. Chapter 4 explained how preferred leader election
keeps that Raft leader aligned with Mako's leader-side submission path. This
chapter addresses the remaining Single-Raft problem: after Raft commits a
record, follower replicas still have to replay that record into their local
database state.

That follower-side work is not a small detail. In Mako, a committed log record
describes the effects of a transaction. Replaying it means applying those
effects to the follower's local state. If many leader-side worker threads can
produce records faster than followers can replay them, the system may appear
fast at the leader while the replicated copies fall behind. ReplayPool is the
mechanism that prevents Single-Raft from hiding that bottleneck.

\section{Why Single-Raft Changes the Bottleneck}

Multi-Raft keeps the same broad shape as the original Paxos backend: different
worker-lane partitions have different replication streams. That design
preserves parallelism in a direct way. Single-Raft deliberately changes the
shape. All worker-lane commands are placed into one consolidated Raft log. This
reduces the amount of Raft machinery and makes the replication topology
simpler, but it also creates fan-in. Many Mako workers can feed one ordered
log.

Fan-in is not automatically bad. Raft may still be able to order the incoming
records quickly. The problem is what happens after ordering. Once an entry is
committed, Mako must deliver it to the correct follower-side replay path. If
that delivery path performs heavy database replay inline, then one narrow
apply-and-replay path can become the limiting stage. More worker threads then
produce more committed records, but follower replay does not necessarily keep
up.

\figref{fig:single-raft-replay-bottleneck} shows the risk. The left side is
leader-side work: multiple Mako workers submit transaction log records. The
middle is the consolidated Raft log: one ordered stream contains records from
many worker lanes. The right side is the follower replay path. Without replay
offload, that path can accumulate backlog. The observed symptom is subtle:
leader-side committed throughput can look healthy even when follower replay
progress is much lower.

The important claim is therefore not that Single-Raft is inherently slow. The
claim is that Single-Raft moves the engineering burden. The consensus log is
consolidated, so replay parallelism has to be preserved somewhere else. If the
implementation measures only leader-side commits, it may miss the real
bottleneck. If it also measures follower replay progress, the bottleneck
becomes visible.

\section{Commit, Apply, and Replay Are Different}

The Single-Raft design is easier to understand if three stages are kept
separate. Table~\ref{tab:replay-stages} names the stages. They often happen
close together in code, but they mean different things for the system.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption[Single-Raft pipeline stages]{Single-Raft pipeline stages and their roles.}
\label{tab:replay-stages}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
Raft commit &
The Raft group has replicated enough log evidence to decide that a record is
part of the agreed order. \\
\addlinespace
Apply staging &
The Raft backend performs lightweight delivery of a committed record into
Mako's callback path, without doing the expensive follower database replay
inline. \\
\addlinespace
Follower replay &
A follower applies the committed transaction record to its local database

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Commit is the consensus decision. It answers: is this record part of the
replicated order? Apply staging is delivery. It answers: how does the committed
record get from Raft back into Mako? Replay is database work. It answers: how
does the follower's local state reflect the committed record?

Conflating these stages creates misleading conclusions. A backend can commit
records quickly but replay them slowly. In that case, the leader appears to
make progress, but the follower copies may fall behind the committed history.
The thesis therefore treats follower replay throughput as a separate sanity
metric. A healthy replicated design needs both committed work and replayed
follower work to move at compatible rates.

\section{Naive Single-Raft Without ReplayPool}

The no-ReplayPool Single-Raft design is useful as a negative control. It shows
what happens if the topology is consolidated but the replay path is not
engineered for that consolidation. The leader-side Mako workers submit records
into the consolidated Raft log. Raft orders and commits those records. After
commit, the apply path delivers each record to Mako, and follower replay work
is performed without a separate replay worker pool.

That design has a simple failure mode. The commit path can keep accepting and
ordering records while the follower-side database work accumulates behind it.
This is especially easy to miss because the benchmark's most visible number is
leader-side committed throughput. If that number is high, the run may look
successful until the replay counters show that followers are processing much
less work.

This negative control is important for the thesis. It prevents the evaluation
from treating a high leader-side number as enough proof. Chapter 8 uses the
no-ReplayPool result to show that Single-Raft must be judged by both commit
throughput and replay progress. It also measures end-of-run replay lag and
drain behavior, so the evaluation can check whether followers actually finish
the records accepted by the leader. The design is not considered healthy
merely because the leader can append and commit records quickly.

\section{ReplayPool Design}

ReplayPool fixes the bottleneck by moving heavy follower-side replay out of
the narrow Raft apply path. In the final pipeline, Mako workers submit records,
Raft commits those records, the Raft-side delivery path hands committed records
to Mako, Mako places the replay work in a queue, and ReplayPool workers perform
the heavy follower-side replay.

Figure~\ref{fig:replaypool-pipeline} shows this final pipeline. Raft still
decides the committed order. The apply path still receives committed records
and delivers them into Mako's callback path. The difference is that this
delivery step does not perform all heavy follower replay inline. Instead, it
copies the committed payload into replay work and places that work in
ReplayPool. A replay worker later consumes the work item and applies the
committed record to the follower's local database state.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/manual/fig05_replay_pool_pipeline.pdf}
\caption[ReplayPool pipeline]{Final Single-Raft replay pipeline. The Raft apply path stages committed payloads, while ReplayPool workers perform heavy follower-side replay in parallel.}
\label{fig:replaypool-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The copy into a replay work item is a practical separation of ownership. The
Raft-side delivery path should be able to move on after it has handed the
committed record to Mako. The replay worker needs a stable copy of the record
because it may execute later, after the delivery path has returned. This turns
replay into queued work instead of inline apply-path work.

ReplayPool is process-local. Each process has its own pool of replay workers,
and each follower-side committed record is routed to one of those workers. The
routing uses the partition-lane identifier carried by the committed command.
Records from the same lane are sent to the same replay worker, so one lane's
order is not split across multiple workers. Records from different lanes can
go to different replay workers and make progress in parallel.

The evaluation uses a configuration called 1:1 ReplayPool. In this thesis,
1:1 means that the follower side is given one replay worker for each
leader-side Mako worker thread in the benchmark run. The phrase describes the
amount of replay parallelism, not the Raft topology. It does not create one
Raft group, one Raft replica, or one Raft apply thread for each Mako worker.
It simply gives follower replay enough parallel workers to match the
leader-side transaction-execution width.

\section{Preserving Correctness While Parallelizing Replay}

Parallel replay has to be disciplined. It is not enough to place committed
records into a thread pool and let any worker apply any record. Mako's state is
partitioned into lanes, and each lane has an ordered history of committed
records. ReplayPool preserves that lane order by routing all records for the
same lane to the same replay worker. In implementation terms, the lane
identifier chooses the replay worker, so records for one lane are queued behind
earlier records for that same lane. At the same time, different lanes can be
replayed by different workers, which recovers the parallelism that Single-Raft
removed from the consensus topology.

The design relies on a simple invariant. Raft decides the committed sequence of
records. Mako then applies each committed record only on a replay path that is
allowed to update the affected local state. For records in the same lane, that
means preserving their lane order. For records in different lanes, parallel
execution is allowed only because Mako already has replay checks that decide
whether a record is safe to apply. ReplayPool is safe in this thesis because it
uses those Mako rules; it is not a generic rule that every Raft application can
apply committed entries in arbitrary parallel order.

ReplayPool also handles records that are committed but not yet safe for the
local replay state to apply. Mako uses progress checks to avoid applying a
record before the local replay path is ready for it. When such a record
arrives, the replay worker defers it rather than discarding it or applying it
early. Deferred records are retried after later progress makes them safe. This
keeps the replay pipeline from violating Mako's local ordering and visibility
requirements.

The correctness boundary is therefore clear. Raft still decides the replicated
log order. ReplayPool preserves that consensus decision and changes only where
follower-side database replay executes. Heavy replay work moves from a narrow
apply path into parallel replay workers, while lane-level order and safety
checks remain in force. The evaluation's replay-throughput and replay-lag
counters then measure whether followers keep up with the committed work under
that design.

\section{Caveat: Global Replay Completion Order}

ReplayPool has an important caveat: it does not preserve the global Raft log
order as the wall-clock order of replay completion. Raft still decides the
official committed order, but ReplayPool can apply independent lanes in
parallel. For example, suppose Raft commits entry 1 for lane A and then entry 2
for lane B. Raft's committed order is still entry 1 before entry 2, but
ReplayPool may send the two entries to different replay workers. If the lane-B
worker runs first, entry 2 may finish replay before entry 1.

That behavior would be the wrong design for a replicated state machine that
requires every committed entry to be applied globally in exact Raft-log order.
ReplayPool is therefore a Mako-specific optimization: it is appropriate here
because Mako's application layer already has rules for parallel transaction
execution and parallel replay.

Mako can tolerate this parallelism because records carry the partition-lane and
timestamp information needed by the replay path. Replay workers use Mako's
watermark checks to decide whether a committed record is safe to apply. If the
record is ahead of the follower's safe watermark, the replay worker keeps it in
a deferred queue and retries it later instead of applying it early. Mako's
underlying concurrency-control mechanisms also use atomic compare-and-swap
style checks to avoid blindly overwriting state when another thread has changed
the expected version first. In other words, Raft supplies the committed order,
while Mako is responsible for making parallel replay safe for its own
partitioned data model. This thesis uses those Mako transaction semantics as
the application-layer contract that makes parallel replay a valid integration
strategy.

\section{Why ReplayPool Is a Systems Contribution}

Consensus protocols are often described as if applying a committed command to
the state machine were cheap. That assumption is not safe for Mako. A committed
command is a transaction log record, and replaying it can mutate database
state. The cost is real CPU work on follower replicas.

ReplayPool is therefore not a cosmetic optimization. It is the mechanism that
makes Single-Raft a viable Mako design rather than only a simpler-looking
replication topology. Single-Raft reduces the number of Raft logs, but that
reduction removes one source of natural parallelism. ReplayPool reintroduces
parallelism where Mako needs it: in follower-side replay.

This is also why the evaluation in Chapter 8 reports more than leader-side
throughput. The thesis compares committed throughput with follower replay
throughput and CPU role measurements. Those metrics show whether a backend is
only committing records at the leader or whether follower replicas are also
making comparable replay progress. ReplayPool is successful when the
Single-Raft run looks healthy under that stricter interpretation.

\section{What Chapter 5 Establishes}

Single-Raft is attractive because it consolidates replication into one ordered
Raft log. That consolidation, however, shifts pressure onto the path that
delivers and replays committed records. A high leader-side commit rate is not
enough if followers replay far more slowly.

ReplayPool is the Single-Raft mechanism that addresses this pressure. It keeps
Raft-side delivery lightweight, copies committed records into replay work
items, routes each partition lane to a stable replay worker, and lets different
lanes replay in parallel. The caveat is that parallel replay relies on Mako's
own lane routing, watermark checks, and atomic update discipline; it is not a
generic promise that any Raft state machine can replay committed entries in
parallel. With preferred leadership from Chapter 4 and ReplayPool from this
chapter, Single-Raft has the systems structure needed for the evaluation in
Chapter 8.
