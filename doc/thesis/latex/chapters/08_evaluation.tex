\chapter{Evaluation}

This chapter evaluates the thesis claim through a sequence of focused
questions. First, can Raft stay in the same performance class as Mako's
original Paxos backend when persistence is disabled? Second, does Single-Raft
remain credible as a replicated design when follower replay is measured, not
only leader-side throughput? Third, does ReplayPool explain the Single-Raft
result by improving follower replay progress? Finally, when persistence cost is
modeled, are the disk results backed by measured storage work rather than by
throughput curves alone?

The figures and the numbers quoted in this chapter come from the same archived
result data. This keeps the prose and graphs tied to the same benchmark runs.

\section{Methodology}

All evaluated backends run under the same benchmark harness and the same TPC-C
workload. The sweep varies the number of Mako worker threads from one to eleven.
The main in-memory comparison includes four modes: Single-Raft without
ReplayPool, Single-Raft with ReplayPool, Multi-Raft, and the original Paxos
backend.

The headline throughput metric is leader-side committed throughput. It measures
how many transactions per second the submission-side benchmark process reports
as committed. That number is necessary, but it is not sufficient for this
thesis. A replicated system can look fast at the leader while followers lag
behind, so the evaluation also reports follower replay progress. Worker CPU is
used as supporting evidence that Mako's transaction workers are active. In the
persistence experiments, simulated-disk counters report modeled bytes, modeled
writes, and normalized bytes per committed transaction. The simulated-disk
settings used for those experiments are the No simulated disk, NVMe-like, and
Cloud-SSD-like settings listed in Table~\ref{tab:simulated-disk-models}.

\section{No-Disk Scalability}

The first experiment asks whether Raft can match Paxos when simulated disk
persistence is disabled. This isolates the replication and replay path from
modeled storage costs. Multi-Raft is the direct Paxos-shaped replacement: it
keeps partition-level replication parallelism. Single-Raft is the consolidated
design: it uses one consolidated Raft log and depends on the replay path to keep
followers close to the committed history.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig07_no_disk_scalability.pdf}
\caption[No-disk scalability]{No-disk scalability. Panel (a) reports leader-side committed throughput. Panel (b) removes the Single-Raft ReplayPool line, exposing the no-ReplayPool replay bottleneck against Multi-Raft and Paxos. Panel (c) adds Single-Raft with ReplayPool back against the same baselines.}
\label{fig:no-disk-scalability}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:no-disk-scalability}(a) shows the headline throughput view. At the
11-worker endpoint, Single-Raft without ReplayPool reports about 593K
leader-side transactions/s. If the evaluation stopped there, the no-ReplayPool
design would look like the best configuration. \figref{fig:no-disk-scalability}(b)
removes the Single-Raft ReplayPool line and shows why that interpretation is
wrong: the no-ReplayPool Single-Raft replay line flattens around 140K
transactions/s, while Multi-Raft and Paxos continue to scale. At the 11-worker
endpoint, no-ReplayPool Single-Raft reaches only about 143K follower replayed
transactions/s. The leader is committing far more work than the follower side
is replaying.

The credible configurations keep leader throughput and follower replay progress
much closer together. \figref{fig:no-disk-scalability}(c) shows the replay path
after ReplayPool is enabled: instead of flattening, the Single-Raft replay line
scales with the worker count and remains close to the same Multi-Raft and Paxos
baselines. At the 11-worker endpoint, Single-Raft with ReplayPool reports about 526K leader-side
transactions/s and about 499K follower replayed transactions/s. Multi-Raft
reports about 510K leader-side transactions/s and about 484K follower replayed
transactions/s. Paxos reports about 472K leader-side transactions/s and about
450K follower replayed transactions/s.

This is the first main evaluation result. Raft is in Paxos's performance class
when the Mako pipeline is preserved. Multi-Raft gets there by preserving the
Paxos-shaped parallel replication structure. Single-Raft gets there only when
ReplayPool prevents follower replay from falling far behind leader-side commit
throughput. Follower replay progress is a progress metric, not a standalone
correctness proof; the correctness argument for parallel replay is the
same-lane ordering, watermark, and atomic-update argument from Chapters 5 and 7.

\section{Worker CPU}

The second experiment asks whether the benchmark is doing real transaction
work. CPU utilization does not prove correctness, and it does not replace the
replay checks above. It is useful because very low worker CPU would suggest that
the benchmark is measuring an idle or blocked harness rather than active Mako
transaction execution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig08_worker_cpu_utilization.pdf}
\caption[Worker CPU utilization]{Worker CPU utilization across the no-disk scalability sweep.}
\label{fig:worker-cpu-utilization}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:worker-cpu-utilization} shows that worker CPU remains high through
the upper end of the sweep. At the 11-worker endpoint, the mean worker CPU is
about 166\% for Single-Raft with ReplayPool, about 253\% for Multi-Raft, and
about 222\% for Paxos. The exact CPU values should not be read as a correctness
claim. They support a narrower interpretation: the throughput curves are
produced while Mako's transaction workers are active, not while the system is
mostly idle.

\section{ReplayPool Mechanism}

The no-disk result shows that ReplayPool matters. This experiment isolates why
it matters. It keeps the Single-Raft design fixed and varies the number of
follower replay workers. The question is not whether adding replay workers
always increases leader-side throughput. The question is whether enough replay
workers are needed for followers to keep up with the committed history.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig09_replay_pool_matrix.pdf}
\caption[ReplayPool sensitivity]{ReplayPool sensitivity across worker counts and replay-pool sizes. Panel (a) reports leader-side throughput, panel (b) reports follower replay progress, and panel (c) normalizes replay progress by leader throughput.}
\label{fig:replaypool-matrix}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:replaypool-matrix}(a) shows the first key pattern. At the 11-worker
endpoint, leader-side throughput stays high across the tested replay-pool
sizes, ranging from about 563K to 584K transactions/s. A graph that only showed
leader throughput would therefore miss the bottleneck.

\figref{fig:replaypool-matrix}(b) reveals the bottleneck. With zero replay
workers, the 11-worker run reaches only about 137K follower replayed
transactions/s. With two replay workers, replay progress improves to about 257K
transactions/s. With four replay workers, it reaches about 461K transactions/s.
With eight and eleven replay workers, it reaches about 541K and 534K
transactions/s, respectively. The useful effect of ReplayPool is therefore
replay completeness: followers track the leader's committed history much more
closely when enough replay workers are available.

\figref{fig:replaypool-matrix}(c) shows the same mechanism below the largest
endpoint. At six Mako worker threads, zero or one replay worker keeps only
about 40\% of the leader's committed work replayed on the follower. Two replay
workers raise that to about 79\%, while four or more replay workers keep replay
progress near 95\%. The needed replay parallelism grows with leader-side worker
parallelism.

This is why the main Single-Raft result uses a 1:1 ReplayPool setting. In this
thesis, 1:1 means the number of follower replay workers equals the number of
Mako transaction workers in the run. It does not mean one Raft group per worker
or one Raft apply thread per worker. It is a conservative setting that keeps
follower replay close to leader-side committed throughput across the tested
sweep.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig10_replay_pool_matrix_heatmap.pdf}
\caption[Follower replay completeness]{Follower replay completeness across worker counts and replay-pool sizes.}
\label{fig:replaypool-heatmap}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:replaypool-heatmap} summarizes the same evidence as replay
completeness. The low-completeness region appears when worker count is high and
the replay pool is too small. The high-completeness region appears when the
replay pool is large enough to absorb follower-side replay work. The mechanism
claim is therefore precise: ReplayPool does not make leader throughput
monotonically increase; it makes Single-Raft credible by preventing follower
replay from becoming the hidden bottleneck.

\section{Simulated Persistence Throughput}

The next experiment adds modeled persistence cost. The goal is not to claim the
absolute performance of a particular physical SSD. Chapter 6 explained why the
thesis uses controlled simulated-disk models, and
Table~\ref{tab:simulated-disk-models} gives their parameters. Here, the
question is how Paxos, Multi-Raft, and Single-Raft behave under the same
no-disk, NVMe-like, and Cloud-SSD-like conditions.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig11_disk_persistence_throughput.pdf}
\caption[Simulated persistence throughput]{Simulated persistence throughput under no-disk, NVMe-like, and Cloud-SSD-like settings. Panels (a), (b), and (c) show Single-Raft with ReplayPool, Multi-Raft, and Paxos, respectively.}
\label{fig:disk-persistence-throughput}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:disk-persistence-throughput}(a)--(c) show two different regimes for
the three backends. Under the NVMe-like model, the curves remain close to the
no-disk results. At the 11-worker endpoint, Single-Raft with ReplayPool reaches
about 545K transactions/s, Multi-Raft reaches about 509K transactions/s, and
Paxos reaches about 475K transactions/s. This suggests that the NVMe-like
modeled cost does not dominate the high-thread runs.

The Cloud-SSD-like model behaves differently. At high worker counts, all three
backends converge near 340K transactions/s. At the 11-worker endpoint,
Single-Raft with ReplayPool, Multi-Raft, and Paxos all report approximately
340K transactions/s. The convergence matters because it is not a separation
between Raft and Paxos. Under this model, the shared storage pressure becomes
the limiting factor.

\section{Simulated-Disk Proof}

The Cloud-SSD result would be weak if the thesis only showed a flattening
throughput curve. A flat line could come from a benchmark artifact, a parsing
mistake, or an unrelated bottleneck. The simulated-disk proof statistics make
the result defensible by showing how much modeled storage work was charged.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig12_simulated_disk_proof_cloudssd.pdf}
\caption[Cloud-SSD simulated-disk proof]{Cloud-SSD flattening with simulated-disk proof statistics. Panel (a) shows Cloud-SSD throughput, panel (b) shows persisted bytes, and panel (c) shows bytes per committed transaction.}
\label{fig:simulated-disk-proof-cloudssd}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:simulated-disk-proof-cloudssd} pairs the Cloud-SSD throughput
flattening with measured storage counters. \figref{fig:simulated-disk-proof-cloudssd}(a)
shows the flattening, while \figref{fig:simulated-disk-proof-cloudssd}(b) shows
that the backends write comparable persisted bytes. At the 11-worker endpoint,
all three backends write about 7.49 GB through the simulated disk and perform
about 24.6K modeled writes. The storage work is therefore comparable across the
backends when their throughput curves converge.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\linewidth]{figures/graphs/fig13_cloudssd_bytes_per_txn.pdf}
\caption[Cloud-SSD bytes per transaction]{Cloud-SSD simulated-disk bytes per committed transaction.}
\label{fig:cloudssd-bytes-per-txn}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

\figref{fig:cloudssd-bytes-per-txn} normalizes the same storage work by useful
committed transactions. At the 11-worker endpoint, each backend records about
733 simulated-disk bytes per committed transaction. This supports the storage
interpretation: the Cloud-SSD-like flattening occurs while the model is
charging comparable bytes, comparable writes, and comparable normalized storage
work across the three backends.

The claim remains intentionally narrow. These graphs show that the controlled
simulated-storage model was doing measured work and that the three backends
were charged similarly when the Cloud-SSD curves flattened. They do not claim
that every production SSD, filesystem, or cloud storage stack will produce the
same absolute throughput.

\section{Evaluation Summary}

The evaluation supports the thesis in four steps. Multi-Raft validates the
direct Paxos-shaped Raft replacement: when Raft is given similar partition-level
parallelism, it remains in the same performance class as Paxos. Both Raft
topologies rely on preferred leader placement to align Raft leadership with
Mako's leader-side submission path. Single-Raft then has one additional
requirement: ReplayPool must keep follower replay close to the committed
history after replication has been consolidated into one Raft log.

The most important measurement lesson is that leader throughput alone is not
enough. Single-Raft without ReplayPool looks fast at the leader but falls far
behind on follower replay. ReplayPool should therefore be judged by follower
replay progress and replay completeness, not by leader throughput alone.

The persistence experiments add a separate lesson: disk claims need storage
proof. Under the Cloud-SSD-like model, throughput flattens near 340K
transactions/s for all three backends, and the simulated-disk counters show
about 7.49 GB, about 24.6K writes, and about 733 bytes per committed
transaction at the 11-worker endpoint. That is the evidence needed to explain
the disk result without overstating it as a production SSD evaluation.
